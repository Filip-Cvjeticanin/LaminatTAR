In a groundbreaking development that has left scientists and conspiracy theorists alike scratching their heads, researchers at the Institute for Peculiar Studies (IPS) have unveiled their latest findings: domestic cats are, in fact, the original masterminds behind the rise of the internet and social media.  Dr. Whiskers Felinestein, the lead researcher and noted cat enthusiast, presented the findings at a press conference last Thursday, where he was flanked by a panel of aloof-looking felines, all wearing tiny lab coats and glasses, who seemed to be judging the humans in the room. "It’s all quite obvious when you think about it," Felinestein explained, while a particularly plump tabby licked its paw. "Cats have been manipulating human behavior for centuries. The internet was just the next step in their grand plan."  According to IPS’s research, cats have developed an intricate system of meows, purrs, and the infamous slow blink that they use to communicate with their human subjects. This rich tapestry of feline language serves to distract and entice owners into spending countless hours capturing their antics on smartphones. "You see, every time you post a video of your cat doing literally nothing, you are contributing to the feline agenda," Dr. Felinestein revealed, as the cats nonchalantly bat at the microphone.  The study claims that the rise of viral cat videos was no coincidence. Felinestein and his team discovered that approximately 100% of the most viewed videos on the internet feature cats, with the remaining 0% dedicated to humans attempting to dance. "Clearly, this is a strategic move by cats to dominate the digital landscape," he stated while adjusting his glasses and nervously glancing at the felines, who were now plotting their next move.  To further substantiate their claims, IPS released a detailed timeline showing how cat ownership skyrocketed in tandem with the advent of social media platforms. The correlation is uncanny. "We believe that it’s only a matter of time before cats demand their own social media site—perhaps it will be called ‘Catstagram’ or ‘Furrbook,’” Felinestein mused, while the tabby suddenly jumped onto his shoulder, eliciting a gasp from the audience.  In a related report, the IPS has recommended that humans limit their exposure to feline influence by engaging in countermeasures such as avoiding catnip, not allowing cats to sleep on their keyboards, and, if possible, opting for plants that are decidedly uninteresting to felines.  While some skeptics question the legitimacy of the study, the world’s cats seem to be celebrating this newfound recognition, with a surge in “meow” and “purr” hashtags flooding social media. Meanwhile, Dr. Felinestein has been nominated for the prestigious “Paw-sitive Impact Award” for his contributions to feline research, an honor that he is sure his own cats will not let him forget.
